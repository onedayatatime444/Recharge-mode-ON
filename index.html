What 62 students told us

An anonymous online survey of students aged 11–18 about sleep, phones and how they feel in lessons.

Method

  • Anonymous online survey, 62 responses.
  • Ages: 11–13 (48.4%), 14–15 (22.6%), 16–18 (29.0%).
  • Eight questions on sleep hours, phone use before bed, where the phone sleeps, staying up late, morning tiredness, concentration and willingness to try a curfew.
  • Percentages come from the survey summary. Student counts are worked out from those percentages.

What the data can’t tell us

  • Not proof of cause. Both late phone use and short sleep are common, but these charts look at each question separately. They don’t show that one causes the other.
  • Self-reported. Students estimated their own sleep and phone use.
  • One group, one moment. 62 students at a single point in time. It isn’t a random sample of all students.
  • Phones aren’t the whole story. About 35% say they rarely or never use their phone before bed, yet only about 15% reach 8 hours. Homework, early starts and other habits matter too.

Science

Why you can’t sleep

What researchers know about teen sleep: the body clock, common sleep disorders, and the studies behind this project.

Please read: this page is for learning, not diagnosis. I’m a student, not a doctor. If sleep problems last for weeks or affect your school, mood or safety, talk to a parent, school nurse or doctor.

Why it’s hard to fall asleep

Teen sleep trouble usually has more than one cause. Tap a card to read more about each one.

A later body clock

During puberty the body clock tends to shift later, so feeling awake at 11pm is often biology, not laziness.

What happens. Your body has an internal clock that runs on a roughly 24-hour rhythm and manages when you feel awake and when you are ready for sleep. Light, darkness and other cues from the environment help keep it in time. When it gets dark, your body releases a hormone called melatonin, which helps you feel sleepy.

What changes in teens. NHLBI says teens fall asleep later at night than younger children and adults, partly because melatonin is released and peaks later in the 24-hour cycle for teens. So it is natural for many teens to prefer later bedtimes and to sleep later in the morning. The authors of an NIH-funded school study described the natural sleep period of teens as starting about two hours later than that of younger children.

A second process. Sleep pressure also builds the longer you are awake, and it peaks in the evening. A compound called adenosine rises in your brain while you are awake and signals a shift toward sleep. Early alarms and late body clocks can pull against each other.

Keep in mind. A later body clock is normal. If it is so late that you can’t fall asleep at a reasonable time for weeks, see the delayed sleep phase card below.

Sources: NHLBI, what makes you sleep · NICHD summary of Widome et al.

Screens and light

Bright screens late in the evening can disrupt melatonin, and bedtime phone use is linked to less sleep.

The light. NHLBI says exposure to bright artificial light late in the evening can disrupt the melatonin process, making it hard to fall asleep. Examples include light from a TV screen or a computer screen.

The research. A review of 20 studies covering about 125,000 children and teens (Carter et al., 2016) found that bedtime use of a media device, and even having one in the bedroom, went with less sleep, poorer sleep quality and more daytime sleepiness. A survey of 9,846 Norwegian 16 to 19-year-olds (Hysing et al., 2015) found that using devices before bed, and heavy daytime screen use, were each linked to too little sleep, and that most used a device within an hour of going to sleep.

What the advice is. The CDC says to turn off electronic devices at least 30 minutes before bedtime, which is the 30 minutes the phone-off countdown on this site uses. NHLBI also advises avoiding TV and devices in the bedroom, since their light can disrupt the sleep-wake cycle.

Keep in mind. These studies show links, not proof that screens cause short sleep, and what you do on the screen may matter as much as the light. My own survey has the same limit.

Sources: NHLBI, what makes you sleep · summary of Carter et al. · Hysing et al. · CDC, about sleep

Caffeine and energy drinks

Caffeine late in the day can make it harder to fall asleep, and teen studies link energy drinks to shorter sleep.

What the health sources say. NHLBI advises avoiding caffeine, nicotine and alcohol close to bedtime because they can make it more difficult to fall asleep. The CDC says to avoid caffeine in the afternoon or evening.

What teen studies find. A 2024 systematic review of 18 studies, covering about 188,000 teens in 13 countries, linked energy drink use to poorer sleep quality and shorter sleep. Another review of 16 papers found caffeine use mostly went with shorter total sleep in teens, while findings on bedtime and how long it took to fall asleep were mixed.

Keep in mind. Most of this research is based on questionnaires, so it shows a link, not proof of cause. Tired teens may also reach for caffeine more, so the link can run both ways. I read these reviews in summary form, not the full papers.

Sources: NHLBI, insomnia treatment · CDC, about sleep · review of energy drinks and teen sleep

Early school starts

An early alarm can cut sleep short for teens whose body clocks run late.

The study. An NIH-funded study (Widome et al., 2020, JAMA Pediatrics) followed 455 students in the Minneapolis–St Paul area from grade 9 to grade 11. Students at schools that started roughly an hour later slept an average of 41 minutes more per night by 10th grade and 43 minutes more by 11th grade than students at schools that did not change.

What stood out. The later-start students did not fall asleep any later, on average. They also slept less on weekend nights than the early-start students, which suggests the early-start students had more sleep to catch up on after the school week.

What it means. The authors concluded that a later school start could help improve sleep deficiency among teens, and they called for more research on effects on driving safety, school results, mental health, attendance and obesity. The 2014 AAP report also discusses later start times as one way to reduce teen sleepiness.

Sources: NICHD summary of Widome et al. · AAP report, 2014

Irregular timing

Big changes in when you sleep from day to day make it harder to keep a steady rhythm.

What the advice says. NHLBI advises going to sleep and waking up around the same time each day, even on weekends, and avoiding a schedule that changes. The CDC gives the same advice: go to bed and get up at the same time every day. Your body clock follows cues like light and dark, so a steady routine gives it steady cues.

What research shows. The 2014 AAP report links later bedtimes and wake-up times at weekends to a bigger sleep debt on weekdays. In the school start study, students who had to wake early on school days slept longer on weekend nights, which is the catch-up pattern.

Keep in mind. Small changes are fine. The advice is to keep your wake-up time fairly steady, and the 7-day challenge in the tools helps you try it.

Sources: NHLBI, insomnia treatment · CDC, about sleep · AAP report, 2014

Stress and worry

A busy or anxious mind makes it harder to switch off, and worrying about sleep can make it worse.

What the sources say. NHLBI lists stress or worry about school, work or relationships as a risk factor for insomnia. It also says that worrying about whether you will get enough sleep, and watching the clock, can raise the risk of insomnia or make it worse.

What helps. NHLBI suggests learning new ways to manage stress and following a routine that helps you wind down, such as reading a book, listening to soothing music or taking a hot bath. CBT-I, a talking therapy for long-term insomnia, includes help to feel less nervous about not being able to sleep, and relaxation techniques.

Keep in mind. Stress is one factor among several. If worry is keeping you awake most nights for weeks, talk to a parent, school nurse or doctor.

Sources: NHLBI, insomnia causes · NHLBI, insomnia treatment

Bedroom and daily habits

The room, the timing of food and exercise, and naps all play a part.

The bedroom. NHLBI says to sleep in a cool, quiet, dark place. Noise or light during the night, and uncomfortably high or low temperatures, can disturb your sleep-wake cycle.

The day. NHLBI suggests getting regular physical activity during the day, at least 5 to 6 hours before bed, because exercising close to bedtime can make it harder to fall asleep. It also suggests avoiding naps, especially in the afternoon, eating meals on a regular schedule, avoiding late-night dinners, and limiting how much you drink close to bedtime. The CDC adds avoiding large meals and alcohol before bed.

Keep in mind. This is general advice for people of all ages, from NHLBI and the CDC, not teen-specific. Pick one or two to try, not all at once.

Sources: NHLBI, insomnia treatment · NHLBI, insomnia causes · CDC, about sleep

Common teen sleep disorders

Most tired teens don’t have a disorder, but some do. Here are four that come up often. Tap a card to learn more.

Insomnia

Trouble falling asleep, staying asleep or waking too early, even when you have the time and a good place to sleep.

What it is. Insomnia is a common sleep disorder. You may have trouble falling asleep, staying asleep, or getting good quality sleep, even with the time and the right environment to sleep well, and it can leave you sleepy in the day. Short-term insomnia often comes from stress or a change in routine and lasts days or weeks. Chronic insomnia happens 3 or more nights a week, lasts more than 3 months, and can’t be fully explained by another health problem.

What it can look like. Some people lie awake for a long time before falling asleep. Others wake often in the night or wake too early and can’t get back to sleep. Poor sleep can mean waking up unrefreshed, feeling sleepy in the day, having trouble focusing, and feeling anxious, down or easily annoyed. Estimates of how many teens have insomnia vary a lot with how it is defined: roughly one in five report symptoms in some national surveys, while a formal diagnosis is much rarer.

What can set it off. Stress or worry, a changing routine, caffeine, nicotine or alcohol, long naps, little physical activity, and using screens close to bedtime can all raise the risk. Worrying about whether you will sleep, and watching the clock, can make it worse. Genes may play a part, because insomnia sometimes runs in families.

What can help. Healthy sleep habits come first: a cool, quiet, dark room, the same wake-up time even at weekends, no caffeine near bedtime, regular daytime activity, and a relaxing wind-down. For long-term insomnia, the usual first treatment is CBT-I, a 6 to 8 week programme run by a doctor, nurse or therapist that you can do in person, by phone or online. NHLBI notes that research has not proven melatonin supplements work for insomnia, so talk to a doctor before taking sleep aids.

See someone if trouble sleeping happens most nights for weeks, or it affects school, mood or safety. This NHLBI information is general, not written only for teens.

Sources: NHLBI, what is insomnia · causes · treatment

Delayed sleep phase

The body clock runs so late that falling asleep early is very hard and mornings are painful, even though sleep itself is normal once it happens.

What it is. Delayed sleep-wake phase disorder is a circadian rhythm disorder. The body’s internal clock is shifted two or more hours later than social norms, so you fall asleep late and wake up late. Once you are asleep, sleep is generally normal in amount and quality. The problem is that the clock doesn’t match school or work times.

What it can look like. The main signs are difficulty falling asleep until late at night and a very hard time getting out of bed for school. That makes it easy to mistake for insomnia, and like insomnia it can cause sleepiness and tiredness in the day. A typical sleep diary shows short sleep on school nights and long sleep at weekends.

How common is it? It is much more common in teens and young adults. AASM says about 7% to 16% of them may have it. In our Norwegian study of 10,220 16 to 18-year-olds, 3.3% met the criteria, so the figure depends on how it is measured. About 40% of people with it have a family history, and evening types, or “night owls”, are more likely to develop it. Too little morning sunlight and too much bright light in the evening may make symptoms worse.

What can help. A doctor will first rule out other causes, such as another sleep disorder, a medical condition, medication or a mental health problem. AASM says carefully timed low-dose melatonin has been shown to work in teens, and its dose and timing should be agreed with a sleep doctor. Bright light soon after waking and avoiding bright light in the evening is also used, although its use for this disorder has not been well validated. Cognitive behavioural therapy can help build habits that support a healthy sleep pattern.

See someone if you can’t fall asleep at a reasonable time for weeks and it is hurting school.

Sources: AASM Sleep Education · Sivertsen et al., 2013

Sleep apnea

Breathing repeatedly stops and starts during sleep, which can leave you tired even after a full night in bed.

What it is. In sleep apnea, breathing repeatedly pauses and restarts during sleep. It can happen at any age. Among children it is most common between ages 2 and 8, but it can also affect older children and teens.

What it can look like. At night: loud snoring, mouth breathing, breathing that starts and stops, gasping, waking often and bedwetting. In the day: sleepiness, trouble paying attention at school, overactivity or behaviour problems, and a dry mouth or headache in the morning. Children with sleep apnea may also have worse asthma.

What causes it. The most common type is obstructive sleep apnea. In children it is often caused by large tonsils or adenoids, and obesity is another common cause. Other risk factors include differences in the face or skull, certain conditions such as Down syndrome, and tobacco smoke.

How it is found and treated. Diagnosis needs a sleep study, and a doctor will look for large tonsils or anything blocking the airway. Treatment depends on the cause and may include surgery to remove the tonsils and adenoids, nasal steroid sprays, a breathing machine (CPAP), oral devices, or help with weight. This is for a doctor to decide.

Why it matters. Untreated, it can affect learning and memory, behaviour and growth, and raise the risk of poor school performance and high blood pressure.

See someone if there is regular loud snoring, gasping or breathing pauses, or you feel very tired despite enough time in bed.

Sources: NHLBI, sleep apnea in children · symptoms

Restless legs

An urge to move the legs, often with an uncomfortable feeling, that is worse in the evening and at night and makes it hard to settle.

What it is. Restless legs syndrome is a neurological condition that causes an irresistible urge to move the legs. It is triggered by resting and trying to sleep, and moving the legs gives temporary relief. People describe the feeling as aching, throbbing, pulling, itching, crawling or creeping.

What it can look like. Symptoms start after sitting or lying still, are relieved by moving, and get worse at night, with a symptom-free period in the early morning. People commonly feel them in the late afternoon or evening, and it can make it very hard to fall asleep or get back to sleep. It can bring tiredness, trouble concentrating, and changes in mood.

Who gets it. It can develop at any age, although it most often begins in middle age. The cause is usually unknown. It often runs in families, and low iron levels in the brain may play a part. It can happen alongside sleep deprivation, sleep apnea, and use of caffeine, nicotine or alcohol, and some medicines, including some antihistamines and some antidepressants, can make it worse.

In younger people. It can be hard to diagnose in children, who may not be able to describe it, and it is sometimes mistaken for “growing pains” or ADHD.

What can help. There is no cure, but treatment can help. A doctor first looks at anything making it worse, such as iron deficiency, and low iron is sometimes treated with supplements, so don’t take iron without a doctor’s advice. Lifestyle steps that may help include cutting back on caffeine, nicotine and alcohol, keeping a regular sleep schedule, moderate exercise, and a warm bath or a massage of the legs.

See someone if it happens often and stops you sleeping.

Sources: NINDS, restless legs syndrome

What lack of sleep does

Short sleep affects far more than feeling tired. Tap a card to open it and read more. Each one lists its sources.

Brain

Focus and attention

Tired brains find it harder to concentrate, react quickly and avoid mistakes.

What happens. Sleep deficiency can cause problems with learning, focusing and reacting. People who are short on sleep take longer to finish tasks, have slower reaction times and make more mistakes. In children and teens it can show up as trouble paying attention, and sometimes as being overactive.

How big is it? NHLBI says that after several nights of losing sleep, even just 1 to 2 hours a night, your ability to function suffers as if you had missed a day or two of sleep altogether. Many people don’t realise they are sleep deficient, because they still feel they can function well.

Keep in mind. This comes from sleep research summaries and public health surveys. It shows a clear link, but how much it affects any one person varies.

Sources: NHLBI, how sleep affects your health · CDC, about sleep

Brain

Memory and learning

Sleep is when your brain stores what you learned that day.

What happens. While you sleep, your brain is getting ready for the next day. It forms new pathways that help you learn and remember information. Studies show a good night’s sleep improves learning and problem-solving, and helps you pay attention, make decisions and be creative.

When sleep is short. Being sleep deficient makes it harder to make decisions, solve problems and remember things, and to manage your emotions and cope with change.

Keep in mind. This is a general finding about people of all ages. It explains why cramming all night tends to backfire.

Sources: NHLBI, how sleep affects your health · CDC, about sleep

Brain

Grades

Short sleep in teens is linked to lower grades and more stress.

What the sources say. NHLBI says children and teens who are sleep deficient may get lower grades and feel stressed. CDC data on middle and high school students, summarised by APTA, lists poor academic performance among the problems linked to short sleep, along with attention and behaviour problems and injuries.

Why it might happen. Sleep affects attention, memory and mood, which all matter in class. Untreated sleep disorders such as sleep apnea are also linked to poor school performance.

Keep in mind. This is a link, not proof of cause. School results depend on many things, and I did not find a figure for how much sleep changes grades, so I have not put one here.

Sources: NHLBI, how sleep affects your health · CDC teen data, summarised by APTA

Mood

Mood and mental health

Sleep loss makes it harder to manage emotions and is linked to low mood.

What happens. Sleep deficiency changes activity in parts of the brain that handle emotions. Children and teens who are sleep deficient may feel angry and impulsive, have mood swings, feel sad or down, or lack motivation. NHLBI says sleep deficiency has also been linked to depression and to risk-taking behaviour. Insomnia itself can make people feel anxious, depressed or easily annoyed.

The other side. Good sleep helps reduce stress and improve mood, according to the CDC. The 2014 AAP report also lists depression among the consequences of too little sleep in teens.

Keep in mind. A link doesn’t show which causes which. If you feel sad or low for weeks, tell a parent, school nurse, doctor or someone else you trust.

Sources: NHLBI, how sleep affects your health · CDC, about sleep · AAP report, 2014

Safety

Drowsy driving

Sleepy drivers react slower and may not realise how impaired they are.

What happens. Sleepy drivers may feel able to drive, yet NHLBI says studies show sleep deficiency harms driving ability as much as, or more than, being drunk. It estimates that driver sleepiness is a factor in about 100,000 car accidents a year in the US, resulting in about 1,500 deaths.

Why teens matter. The 2014 AAP report lists car crashes caused by drowsiness among the main consequences of insufficient sleep in teens and young adults, and discusses later school start times as one way to reduce sleepiness. The CDC lists a lower risk of crashes among the benefits of enough sleep.

Keep in mind. The accident figures are estimates for the whole US population, not just teens. The safe rule is simple: if you are very sleepy, don’t drive.

Sources: NHLBI, how sleep affects your health · AAP report, 2014 · CDC, about sleep

Brain

Microsleeps

Your brain can switch off for a few seconds while your eyes are open.

What it is. Lack of sleep can lead to microsleeps, which are brief moments of sleep that happen when you are normally awake. You can’t control them and you might not know they are happening.

What it looks like. Have you ever driven somewhere and not remembered part of the trip? That may be a microsleep. In a lesson, you might miss some of what the teacher said, or feel like you don’t understand the point, because part of it was slept through without you noticing.

Keep in mind. This is a description from NHLBI of how sleep loss affects daytime alertness. It explains why a tired student can sit through a whole lesson and still feel lost.

Sources: NHLBI, how sleep affects your health

Brain

Risk-taking

Sleepier teens are more likely to take risks, especially behind the wheel.

What the research says. NHLBI says sleep deficiency has been linked to risk-taking behaviour, and that being sleep deficient makes it harder to make decisions and control your behaviour. In US survey data on teens, those who slept 7 hours or less on school nights were more likely to report risky driving behaviour, such as not wearing a seatbelt or riding with a driver who had been drinking.

Keep in mind. Surveys like this show a link between short sleep and risky behaviour, not proof that sleep loss causes it. The survey figures here come from news coverage of the CDC data, not the original report.

Sources: NHLBI, how sleep affects your health · news report on CDC data (CBC)

Life

Sleep debt adds up

A little lost sleep every night, or a big weekend lie-in, adds up.

What happens. NHLBI says that after several nights of losing sleep, even a loss of just 1 to 2 hours a night, your ability to function suffers as if you hadn’t slept for a day or two. Many people don’t realise this is happening to them.

The weekend effect. The 2014 AAP report links later bedtimes and wake-up times at weekends to a bigger sleep debt on weekdays. A sleep diary of a teen with a late body clock often shows short sleep on school nights and long sleep at weekends, a pattern AASM describes for delayed sleep phase.

What it means. Keeping your wake-up time fairly steady across the week helps, which is why the 7-day challenge in the tools focuses on a regular routine.

Sources: NHLBI, how sleep affects your health · AAP report, 2014 · AASM Sleep Education

Safety

Sports injuries

Teen athletes who slept under 8 hours were about 1.7 times as likely to be injured.

The study. Milewski and colleagues (2014, Journal of Pediatric Orthopaedics) surveyed 112 athletes averaging 15 years old. Those who slept under 8 hours a night were about 1.7 times as likely to have had an injury as those who slept 8 or more. Hours of sleep and grade in school were the best predictors of injury.

Keep in mind. The result was close to the edge of statistical significance (95% range 1.0 to 3.0), and it came from a survey, so it shows a link only. A later review found chronic lack of sleep is linked to more sports injuries in teens, but could not say what one short night does.

Sources: Milewski et al., 2014

Body

Catching colds

Short sleepers were about 4 times as likely to catch a cold in a famous experiment.

The experiment. Prather and colleagues (2015, Sleep) tracked the sleep of 164 adult volunteers with a wrist sensor for a week, then gave them cold virus in nose drops. Those who slept under 6 hours a night were about 4.2 times as likely to catch a cold as those who slept more than 7 hours.

The bigger picture. NHLBI says ongoing sleep deficiency can change how the body’s natural defences respond, so you may have trouble fighting common infections. The CDC lists getting sick less often among the benefits of enough sleep.

Keep in mind. The volunteers were adults, not teens, so this shows what sleep loss can do to people in general.

Sources: UCSF news on Prather et al. · NHLBI, how sleep affects your health · CDC, about sleep

Body

Hunger and weight

Short sleep raises the hunger hormone and lowers the fullness hormone.

The hormones. NHLBI explains that when you don’t get enough sleep, ghrelin, the hormone that makes you feel hungry, goes up, and leptin, the hormone that makes you feel full, goes down, so you feel hungrier than when you are well rested.

The experiment. In a small study by Spiegel and colleagues (2004, Annals of Internal Medicine), 12 healthy young men had short sleep and then normal sleep. After short sleep, ghrelin was about 28% higher, leptin about 18% lower, and hunger about 24% higher, with more appetite for sweet and starchy foods.

Weight. The AAP report lists obesity risk among the consequences of insufficient sleep in teens, and NHLBI-funded research found that adults who regularly get 7 to 8 hours have a lower risk of obesity and high blood pressure.

Keep in mind. The hormone study was small and used only men, and other studies on these hormones have given mixed results. The weight link is a link, not proof of cause.

Sources: Spiegel et al., 2004 · NHLBI, how sleep affects your health · AAP report, 2014

Body

Blood sugar

Short sleep changes how the body responds to insulin.

What happens. Insulin is the hormone that controls your blood sugar. NHLBI says sleep deficiency results in a higher-than-normal blood sugar level, which may raise your risk of diabetes. The CDC lists a lower risk of type 2 diabetes among the benefits of enough sleep.

Teen evidence. A research review describes an experiment in healthy teenage boys: three nights of 4 hours’ sleep made their bodies respond worse to insulin than nights of 9 hours.

Keep in mind. That experiment was small and I read about it in a review, not the original paper. The effect on long-term diabetes risk comes mostly from adult research.

Sources: NHLBI, how sleep affects your health · CDC, about sleep · research review

Body

Heart and blood pressure

Good sleep helps repair the heart and blood vessels.

What happens. NHLBI says good-quality sleep heals and repairs your heart and blood vessels, and lowers the risk of heart disease, high blood pressure, obesity and stroke. NHLBI-funded research found that adults who regularly get 7 to 8 hours of sleep have a lower risk of obesity and high blood pressure.

In teens. Some studies of teens link short sleep or late bedtimes to higher blood pressure, and the Project Viva study of 829 young people found longer, better sleep went with a healthier profile, including lower systolic blood pressure.

Keep in mind. A review of this research says the evidence in teens is limited and fairly weak, most studies use self-reported sleep, and it is unclear which causes which. Most of the strong evidence is from adults.

Sources: NHLBI, how sleep affects your health · Project Viva analysis

Body

Growth and repair

Deep sleep is when the body releases growth hormone.

What happens. In children and teens, sleep supports growth and development. NHLBI says deep sleep triggers the body to release the hormone that promotes normal growth in children and teens. This hormone also boosts muscle mass and helps repair cells and tissues in children, teens and adults. Sleep also plays a role in puberty and fertility.

Keep in mind. This is NHLBI’s summary of how sleep supports the body. It doesn’t mean one short night will stunt growth, but it shows why teens in particular need a good amount of sleep.

Sources: NHLBI, how sleep affects your health

Body

Pain

One sleepless night can lower the point at which things start to hurt.

The experiment. In a UC Berkeley study of 25 healthy young adults, a night without sleep lowered the point at which heat started to feel painful, compared with a full night’s sleep.

More evidence. Research in mice has found that sleep loss raises pain sensitivity, and a review concluded that the total amount of sleep lost is the main driver, not just the loss of one sleep stage.

Keep in mind. The human study was small and in young adults, and most of the detailed mechanism comes from animal studies and is still being worked out.

Sources: UC Berkeley news · review, 2022

The studies

Five pieces of research behind the tips on this site. Each link opens the original paper or a trustworthy summary.

Guidelines · 2016

How much sleep do teens need?

Paruthi et al., Journal of Clinical Sleep Medicine. A panel of 13 sleep experts reviewed 864 published articles and recommended 8 to 10 hours per 24 hours for ages 13 to 18. It is endorsed by the American Academy of Pediatrics. This is the target my bedtime calculator uses.

Read the paper
Screens · 2016

Devices at bedtime

Carter et al., JAMA Pediatrics. A review of 20 studies covering about 125,000 children and teens (ages 6–19) found that bedtime device use or access went with less sleep, poorer sleep quality and more daytime sleepiness. Limit: the studies are observational, so they show a link, not proof of cause.

Read a summary of the study
Screens · 2015

Electronics and sleep in 9,846 teens

Hysing et al., BMJ Open. A survey of Norwegian 16–19-year-olds found that using electronic devices before bed, and heavy daytime screen time, were each linked to getting too little sleep. Most students used a device within an hour of sleeping.

Read the paper
Body clock · 2013

Delayed sleep phase in teens

Sivertsen et al., BMC Public Health. A population study of 10,220 Norwegian 16–18-year-olds. About 3.3% had delayed sleep phase, and it was linked to higher school absence.

Read the paper
School start times · 2020

What happens when school starts later?

Widome et al., JAMA Pediatrics. 455 students in the Minneapolis–St Paul area were followed from grade 9 to 11. Those whose school moved to a later start slept about 43 minutes more per night than students at schools that did not change.

Read the NIH summary

What this means for my project

These studies mostly show links, not proof of cause: teens who use phones late tend to sleep less, but other things could be involved. My survey of 62 students has the same limit, which is why the next step is the 7-day challenge, to test what actually changes when screens go off earlier.

When to ask for help

  • Trouble sleeping most nights for several weeks.
  • Loud snoring, gasping or breathing pauses while asleep.
  • Leg discomfort that stops you settling at night.
  • Sleepiness that affects school, mood or safety, for example when travelling or doing sport.

Start with a parent or carer, your school nurse, or your doctor.

About

About the project

A student project about sleep, phones and what a small change might do.

The problem

Sleep is easy to ignore and hard to fix. Students aged 11–18 generally need around 8 to 10 hours a night, and younger students need more. In my survey of 62 students, only about 1 in 7 reached 8 hours on a school night.

The question

To what extent does phone use in the hour before bed reduce sleep duration and next-day concentration among students, and can a simple curfew change it?

What I did

  1. Surveyed students. An anonymous online survey with 62 responses across ages 11–13, 14–15 and 16–18.
  2. Analysed the answers. I worked out the key percentages and looked at what the data can and can’t show. See the research page.
  3. Built tools. A bedtime calculator, phone-off countdown, sleep score quiz, sleep debt calculator and a 7-day challenge. See the tools.
  4. Designed take-home cards. Six short, printable tips, each with a QR code that links back to this site.

My role

I led this project from the question to the finished tools: I wrote the survey, analysed the responses, and designed and built these tools and this website. Thank you to my school team and to the 62 students who took part.

Limits and next steps

  • The survey shows that short sleep and late phone use are both common. It does not prove that one causes the other.
  • Next, I would run the 7-day challenge with a group and compare sleep and tiredness before and after.
  • I would also ask teachers what tiredness looks like in lessons, to add a second view to the students’ own answers.
  • Finally, I would share the results with the school and ask what it would take to try a school-wide “screens off” reminder.

Privacy and sources

These tools run entirely in your browser. There are no accounts. Quiz answers, sleep hours and challenge ticks stay on your own device. The only thing that is ever sent to me is the optional, anonymous check-in at the end of the 7-day challenge, which asks for no name or email and which you can skip.
